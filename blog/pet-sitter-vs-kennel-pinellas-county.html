Pet Sitter vs Kennel: The Real Cost & Comfort Comparison

Pet Care · Decision Guide · July 25, 2026 · 6 min read

You're heading out of town. Your dog or cat needs care. The two options most people weigh are a boarding kennel or an in-home pet sitter. But the decision isn't just about price — it's about stress, routine, exposure, and what happens when your pet comes home.

Here's the breakdown using real data, real research, and real costs for pet owners in Pinellas County.

In-home care means one-on-one attention — not divided among 20+ animals.

The Stress Factor: What the Research Says

Multiple peer-reviewed studies have measured cortisol — the primary stress hormone — in dogs during kennel stays. The findings are consistent: dogs show a significant increase in cortisol levels when moved from a home environment to a kennel, even for short-term stays.

A comprehensive review published in the NIH's PubMed Central found that "dogs tend to have an initial increase in cortisol levels when" moved into kennels, and that "shelter dogs, particularly those living in high-stress environments like kennels, tend to exhibit elevated cortisol levels compared to pet dogs" living in homes.

The University of Birmingham's Centre for Animal Welfare found that cortisol-to-creatinine ratios (a standard stress marker) were "significantly higher in the kennel compared to the home environment."

Translation: your dog's body is physically reacting to the kennel environment with measurable stress — even if they seem fine on the surface.

What Happens When Your Dog Comes Home from Boarding

According to Friendship Veterinary Hospital, there are five common behavioral changes pet owners see after boarding:

  • Ravenous eating — dogs often eat less in kennels due to stress, then "catch up" when home
  • Upset stomach — temporary colitis from excitement and dietary disruption is common
  • Lethargy and excessive sleeping — the kennel environment is overstimulating, and dogs crash when they get home
  • Increased drinking — pets drink less in unfamiliar settings and compensate at home
  • Clinginess and separation anxiety — many pets become attached at the hip after a kennel stay, showing signs of separation distress

None of this means kennels are inherently bad — quality facilities exist, and some dogs do well in them. But the data is clear that even short-term kenneling is a stress event for most pets.

Same dog, same routine, same toys — no transition stress with in-home care.

Why In-Home Care Wins for Pinellas County Pet Owners

Here's the thing about in-home pet sitting: your pet stays where they're already comfortable. Same bed, same food, same walking route, same couch. No car ride to a facility. No intake process. No contact with other animals and their germs. No adjustment period when you get back.

Cats especially struggle with kennels — territorial animals removed from their space.

The benefits that matter most:

  • Zero transition stress — your pet never leaves home, so cortisol stays at baseline
  • Normal routine maintained — same feeding times, same walk schedule, same medication timing
  • No exposure to other animals — reduces risk of illness, parasites, and behavioral incidents
  • Home security — someone is coming and going, picking up mail, keeping the house looking lived-in
  • One-on-one attention — your pet isn't one of 20+ animals competing for a staff member's time
  • Photo updates — you see your pet every day, not just at pickup
